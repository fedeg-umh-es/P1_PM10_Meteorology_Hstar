\begin{abstract}
\noindent
Accurate multi-horizon forecasting of \PMten{} concentrations is increasingly
important as regulators tighten ambient air quality standards.  This study
investigates whether the addition of concurrent meteorological predictors
extends the useful forecast horizon $H^*$ — defined as the range of lead times
over which a data-driven model reliably outperforms a naïve persistence
benchmark — in an urban \PMten{} context.  We apply a rigorous rolling-origin
expanding-window backtesting protocol (January 2020 -- July 2023) at two
European sites: Madrid Casa de Campo (Spain) and a network of eight monitoring
stations in Ireland.  Three model classes are evaluated at each of $h = 1,
\ldots, 24$ lead hours: (i) a persistence baseline, (ii) SARIMA(1,0,1)(1,0,0)$_{24}$
as a linear statistical reference, and (iii) XGBoost fitted separately for each
horizon, once with \PMten{} lags only (\textit{lags only}) and once with lags
plus nine meteorological covariates (\textit{lags + met.}).  Forecast quality
is measured via the RMSE skill score relative to persistence; $H^*$ is
determined both under a strict criterion (longest consecutive positive-skill run
from $h = 1$) and a relaxed criterion (last horizon with positive skill).

At Madrid, adding meteorology extends $H^*_{\text{strict}}$ from 9~h to 17~h
($\Delta H^* = +8$~h), a gain attributed to the high lag-1 autocorrelation of
the series ($\rho_1 = 0.957$), which sustains persistence as a strong
competitor at short lead times while allowing meteorologically informed models
to maintain skill at medium horizons.  In contrast, the eight Irish stations,
characterised by more moderate mean autocorrelation ($\bar{\rho}_1 = 0.850$),
show mean $\Delta H^*_{\text{strict}} = +0.9$~h, with XGBoost already
extracting most of the predictable signal from lagged \PMten{} alone.
Diebold--Mariano significance testing confirms that the meteorology benefit at
Madrid is statistically significant at $h = 12$ ($p = 0.012$) but not at
shorter or longer horizons.  We interpret the contrast in terms of
boundary-layer dynamics: high $\rho_1$ reflects stable, stagnant
boundary-layer conditions under which aerosol concentrations evolve slowly and
meteorological forcing becomes decisive only beyond $h \approx 9$~h, when
advection and mixing begin to dominate over in-situ accumulation.  These
findings indicate that the value of meteorological covariates in \PMten{}
forecasting is governed by the local boundary-layer persistence regime, and
that $H^*$ provides a compact, operationally meaningful diagnostic of that
dependence.

\medskip
\noindent
\textbf{Keywords:} PM\textsubscript{10}; atmospheric aerosol; boundary layer;
meteorological covariates; forecast horizon; XGBoost; SARIMA; skill score;
Diebold--Mariano test; air quality.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}
% ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

Particulate matter with aerodynamic diameter below 10~\si{\micro\metre}
(\PMten{}) is the dominant coarse aerosol fraction in the urban boundary layer
and one of the most consequential atmospheric pollutants in European cities.
Its surface concentration integrates contributions from primary crustal and
traffic-related emissions, long-range transported dust, and secondary aerosol
formation, all modulated by boundary-layer mixing, wet scavenging, and
synoptic-scale advection \citep{Seinfeld2006, Kumar2016}.  Sustained exposure
is associated with cardiovascular and respiratory morbidity, premature
mortality, and reduced quality of life \citep{WHO2021, EEA2023}.  The European
regulatory framework has progressively tightened in response: Directive
2008/50/EC established the current limit of
40~\si{\micro\gram\per\cubic\metre} as an annual mean, and the revised
Directive 2024/2881/EU, which entered into force in December 2024, halves that
threshold to 20~\si{\micro\gram\per\cubic\metre} by 2030 \citep{EU2024_2881}.
Against this backdrop, accurate \PMten{} forecasts are increasingly valuable
both for triggering episode-specific abatement measures and for guiding
longer-term urban planning.

The temporal evolution of near-surface \PMten{} is tightly coupled to
boundary-layer dynamics.  Under stable nocturnal or anticyclonic conditions,
the mixing layer is shallow and aerosol concentrations build up through
in-situ accumulation, producing high lag-1 autocorrelation.  Conversely,
deep daytime convective mixing, frontal passages, and precipitation scavenging
all accelerate aerosol dispersal, reducing persistence.  Wind speed and
direction govern horizontal transport of aerosol plumes from upwind sources,
while relative humidity influences hygroscopic growth and optical properties
of the coarse fraction \citep{Seinfeld2006, Kumar2016}.  This physical coupling
motivates the hypothesis that incorporating meteorological predictors into
statistical or machine-learning forecasting models should improve forecast
skill, particularly at medium-to-long lead times where the aerosol signal can
no longer be extrapolated from its recent history alone.

Empirical evidence, however, is mixed.  Several studies report clear gains
when meteorological inputs are added to machine-learning forecasting models:
\citet{Pak2018} found substantial improvements from boundary-layer variables
for PM prediction in Korea, and \citet{Bai2018} demonstrated that wavelet
decomposition of meteorological inputs improved short-range pollutant
forecasts in China.  Other studies find that lagged pollutant concentrations
already capture most of the predictable variance, leaving little room for
meteorology to contribute at sub-daily lead times.  This inconsistency across
the literature likely reflects differences in local autocorrelation structure,
data availability, and the choice of forecast horizon — factors that are
rarely disentangled in a single study.

The \emph{forecast horizon problem} is central to practical decision-making in
air quality management.  Operators of early-warning systems need to know not
only whether a model is skilful on average, but over which range of lead times
it reliably outperforms a naïve benchmark.  We formalize this as the
\emph{useful forecast horizon} $H^*$: the lead-time threshold beyond which
model skill relative to persistence becomes negligible.  The central question
of this paper is: by how much, if at all, does the addition of meteorological
covariates extend $H^*$ for \PMten{}, and what site-level characteristics
govern that increment?

To answer this question we conduct a controlled multi-city comparison.
Madrid Casa de Campo (Spain) and a network of eight stations in Ireland provide
contrasting urban environments with different meteorological regimes and
\PMten{} autocorrelation structures.  We evaluate three model classes —
persistence, SARIMA, and XGBoost — under a strict leakage-free
rolling-origin protocol and use the Diebold--Mariano test
\citep{Diebold1995, Harvey1997} to assess whether observed skill differences
are statistically distinguishable.

The remainder of this paper is structured as follows.  Section~\ref{sec:data}
describes the datasets and preprocessing.  Section~\ref{sec:methods} presents
the backtesting protocol, model specifications, and evaluation metrics.
Section~\ref{sec:results} reports skill curves, $H^*$ estimates, and
significance tests for both sites.  Section~\ref{sec:discussion} interprets
the results in terms of the autocorrelation-persistence mechanism.
Section~\ref{sec:conclusions} summarises the main findings and their
operational implications.

\section{Data}
\label{sec:data}
% ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

\subsection{Madrid}

Hourly \PMten{} concentrations and concurrent meteorological measurements were
obtained for Madrid Casa de Campo from the Madrid City Council open-data portal.
The station is classified as an urban-background site on the western edge of
the city, away from major point sources, and is representative of citywide
background conditions.  The study period spans January 2020 through July 2023;
records with missing \PMten{} or meteorological data were flagged and excluded
from model training and evaluation on a per-origin basis.  Nine meteorological
variables were retained: precipitation (\textit{rain}), temperature
(\textit{temp}), wet-bulb temperature (\textit{wetb}), dew-point temperature
(\textit{dewpt}), vapour pressure (\textit{vappr}), relative humidity
(\textit{rhum}), mean sea-level pressure (\textit{msl}), wind speed
(\textit{wdsp}), and wind direction (\textit{wddir}).

The lag-1 autocorrelation of hourly \PMten{} at Madrid over the training
period (2020--2022) is $\rho_1 = 0.957$, indicative of a highly persistent
series dominated by slow, boundary-layer-driven accumulation and dispersal
episodes lasting tens of hours.

\subsection{Ireland}

Hourly \PMten{} concentrations and meteorological covariates were obtained from
the Irish Environmental Protection Agency (EPA) monitoring network.  Eight
stations distributed across Ireland were selected on the basis of data
completeness over the study window (January 2020 -- July 2023):
Birr (Co.\ Offaly), Dublin Airport, Dundalk (Co.\ Louth), Pearse Street
Dublin, Ringsend Dublin, Edenderry (Co.\ Offaly), Henry Street Limerick, and
Portlaoise (Co.\ Laois).  The same nine meteorological variables used for
Madrid were extracted from the nearest EPA synoptic station for each monitoring
site.

Table~\ref{tab:descriptive} summarises the basic \PMten{} statistics for all
nine sites over the training and evaluation periods.  Concentrations are
generally low to moderate, with training-period means ranging from
11.3~\si{\micro\gram\per\cubic\metre} (Portlaoise) to
19.1~\si{\micro\gram\per\cubic\metre} (Dublin Airport), and Madrid at
18.4~\si{\micro\gram\per\cubic\metre}.  The 95th percentile values indicate
that episode concentrations above 40~\si{\micro\gram\per\cubic\metre} occur
at most stations, underscoring the operational relevance of medium-horizon
forecasting.  Data completeness is high: missing rates are below 2\% at all
sites during both periods.

\section{Methods}
\label{sec:methods}
% ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

\subsection{Backtesting protocol}

We adopt a rolling-origin expanding-window backtesting scheme to simulate
operational forecasting while ensuring strict data separation between training
and evaluation sets.  For each forecast origin $t$, the training window covers
all available observations up to (but not including) $t$; no future information
is used at any point.  The forecast origin advances in daily strides of 24~h,
producing one $h = 1, \ldots, 24$ forecast vector per day.

The evaluation period is 1 January 2023 -- 31 July 2023 (31 weeks, yielding
approximately 210--365 origins per station depending on data completeness).
The mandatory minimum training window is 8\,760 observations (one year), so
origins in early 2023 draw on three years of history.  This protocol prevents
any form of look-ahead bias: model selection, hyperparameter choices, and
feature sets were all fixed prior to the evaluation window.

\subsection{Model specifications}

\paragraph{Persistence.}
The persistence model predicts $\hat{y}_{t+h} = y_t$ for all $h$.  It serves
as the denominator benchmark for the skill score.

\paragraph{SARIMA.}
We fit a SARIMA(1,0,1)(1,0,0)$_{24}$ model at each origin using the
\textit{statsmodels} implementation of exact maximum likelihood.  To avoid
numerical instability of the Kalman filter on very long training windows, the
training series is truncated to the most recent 17\,520 observations
(approximately two calendar years) at each origin.  Multi-step-ahead forecasts
are generated by iterating the fitted state-space model forward.  SARIMA is
treated as a univariate statistical reference; it does not receive
meteorological inputs.

\paragraph{XGBoost — lags only.}
A separate XGBoost regressor \citep{Chen2016} is trained for each horizon
$h \in \{1, \ldots, 24\}$ (the \emph{direct} multi-horizon strategy).  The
feature set consists of eight lagged \PMten{} values
($y_{t-1}, y_{t-2}, y_{t-3}, y_{t-6}, y_{t-12}, y_{t-24}, y_{t-48},
y_{t-168}$) and four calendar indicators (hour of day, day of week, month,
Julian day).  Hyperparameters are fixed across all origins and horizons
(Table~\ref{tab:xgb_params}) to ensure fair comparison; no origin-level
cross-validation is performed.

\paragraph{XGBoost — lags + meteorology.}
Identical to the lags-only model except that the nine meteorological variables
measured at time $t$ are appended to the feature vector.  This is an
\emph{observational} setup in which concurrent meteorology is assumed to be
available at the moment of forecast issuance (e.g., from a station collocated
with the \PMten{} sensor).  Because observed meteorological values are used
rather than numerical weather prediction (NWP) forecasts, the reported skill
scores represent an \emph{upper bound} on what an operationally deployed
lags-plus-meteorology system could achieve; the meteorological forecast error
cascade is discussed as a limitation in Section~\ref{sec:discussion}.

\subsection{Evaluation metrics}

\paragraph{Skill score.}
For each model $m$ and horizon $h$, the RMSE skill score relative to
persistence is:
\begin{equation}
  S_m(h) = 1 - \frac{\mathrm{RMSE}_m(h)}{\mathrm{RMSE}_{\text{pers}}(h)},
  \label{eq:skill}
\end{equation}
where the RMSEs are computed over all forecast origins in the evaluation period.
Positive $S_m(h)$ indicates that model $m$ outperforms persistence at horizon
$h$.

\paragraph{Useful forecast horizon $H^*$.}
We define two variants:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{$H^*_{\text{strict}}$}: the length of the longest
    \emph{consecutive} positive-skill run beginning at $h = 1$.  This captures
    the unbroken window of reliable improvement over persistence.
  \item \textbf{$H^*_{\text{relax}}$}: the last horizon at which $S_m(h) > 0$.
    This is more lenient and captures the extent of intermittent skill.
\end{itemize}
The meteorology benefit is $\Delta H^* = H^*_{\text{lags+met}} -
H^*_{\text{lags only}}$ under either criterion.

\paragraph{Diebold--Mariano test.}
Statistical significance of the difference in squared forecast errors between
the lags-only and lags-plus-meteorology conditions is assessed using the
Diebold--Mariano test \citep{Diebold1995} with the finite-sample correction
proposed by \citet{Harvey1997} (DM-HLN).  Tests are conducted at horizons
$h \in \{1, 6, 12, 24\}$, with a two-sided $\alpha = 0.05$ threshold.
Because four tests are conducted simultaneously per site, the family-wise
Bonferroni-corrected threshold is $\alpha_{\text{adj}} = 0.0125$; results are
reported at both the nominal and corrected thresholds.

\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}
% ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

\subsection{Madrid: skill curves and $H^*$}

Figure~\ref{fig:madrid_skill} shows the RMSE skill score $S(h)$ as a function
of lead time for all three model classes at Madrid Casa de Campo.  All three
models outperform persistence at short horizons.  The lags-only XGBoost
achieves positive skill from $h = 1$ through approximately $h = 11$ before
falling below zero, yielding $H^*_{\text{strict}} = 9$~h and
$H^*_{\text{relax}} = 15$~h.  SARIMA achieves $H^*_{\text{strict}} = 8$~h
and $H^*_{\text{relax}} = 20$~h; its skill curve rises more steeply at
intermediate horizons but is less consistent than XGBoost.  Adding
meteorological covariates to XGBoost (\textit{lags + met.}) produces a
markedly different profile: $H^*_{\text{strict}} = 17$~h and
$H^*_{\text{relax}} = 17$~h, reflecting a plateau of positive skill maintained
across the full 1--17~h range.  The gain relative to the lags-only model is
$\Delta H^*_{\text{strict}} = +8$~h.

Figure~\ref{fig:madrid_delta} plots the pointwise skill difference
$\Delta S(h) = S_{\text{lags+met}}(h) - S_{\text{lags only}}(h)$.  The
difference is positive at most horizons but grows substantially between $h = 9$
and $h = 17$, precisely the window where lags-only skill turns negative while
meteorology sustains it.  At $h = 18$ onwards both conditions are negative and
the gain dissipates.

Figure~\ref{fig:madrid_dm} and Table~\ref{tab:madrid_dm} report the DM-HLN
test results at $h \in \{1, 6, 12, 24\}$.  The only statistically significant
difference is at $h = 12$ ($\mathrm{DM} = 2.52$, $p = 0.012$), which
survives the Bonferroni-corrected threshold of $\alpha_{\text{adj}} = 0.0125$
for four simultaneous tests.  Lags-plus-meteorology is favoured at $h = 12$.
At $h = 1$ ($p = 0.243$), $h = 6$ ($p = 0.961$), and $h = 24$ ($p = 0.398$)
the null hypothesis of equal predictive accuracy cannot be rejected.  This
pattern is consistent with the $\Delta S(h)$ profile: the meteorology benefit
is concentrated in the mid-range, medium-term window.

\subsection{Ireland: station-level $H^*$ and meteorology benefit}

Figure~\ref{fig:ireland_skill} shows the RMSE skill curves for all eight Irish
stations as a 2$\times$4 panel grid.  A common pattern is visible: both the
lags-only and lags-plus-meteorology XGBoost models achieve positive skill across
most or all of the 1--24~h horizon at the majority of stations, in marked
contrast to Madrid where skill turned negative beyond $h \approx 9$--$11$.
The two curves are largely overlapping at most stations, foreshadowing the small
$\Delta H^*$ values reported below.

Figure~\ref{fig:ireland_delta} plots the pointwise meteorology benefit
$\Delta S(h) = S_{\text{lags+met}}(h) - S_{\text{lags only}}(h)$ for each
station.  Values cluster near zero across the full horizon range for most
stations, confirming that the lags-only model already captures the dominant
predictable variance.  Henry Street Limerick stands out as the station with
the largest positive $\Delta S$ at intermediate horizons, consistent with its
being the only station to show a notable $\Delta H^*_{\text{strict}} = +6$~h.

Table~\ref{tab:ireland_hstar} reports $H^*_{\text{strict}}$ and
$H^*_{\text{relax}}$ for all eight Irish stations and all four model classes.
A striking feature is that both XGBoost conditions achieve near-maximum
$H^*_{\text{strict}}$ at most stations (six of eight stations reach 24~h with
lags-only alone), leaving little room for meteorology to contribute.

The mean $\Delta H^*_{\text{strict}}$ across the eight stations is
$\bar{\Delta H^*} = +0.9$~h, compared with $+8$~h at Madrid.  The two
exceptions are Dublin Airport ($\Delta H^*_{\text{strict}} = +1$~h) and Henry
Street Limerick ($\Delta H^*_{\text{strict}} = +6$~h); at the remaining six
stations the increment is zero.  SARIMA performance is more variable: mean
$H^*_{\text{strict}} = 14.3$~h, with a low of 4~h at Henry Street Limerick and
a high of 24~h at Dublin Airport.  Figure~\ref{fig:ireland_hstar} presents
these results as a grouped bar chart, facilitating direct station-by-station
comparison.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}
% ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

\subsection{The autocorrelation-persistence mechanism}

The central contrast between Madrid and Ireland — $\Delta H^*_{\text{strict}}
= +8$~h vs.\ $+0.9$~h — can be parsimoniously explained by the strength of
lag-1 autocorrelation in each series.  At Madrid ($\rho_1 = 0.957$) the
\PMten{} signal is highly persistent: the naïve persistence forecast is
difficult to beat at short lead times ($h = 1$--$8$) because the concentration
one hour hence is almost identical to the concentration now.  Only at
$h \ge 9$, when the persistence signal has sufficiently decayed, can
meteorology provide information that exceeds what the lagged \PMten{} series
alone could supply.  The lags-only XGBoost, unable to leverage external
forcing, exhausts its predictive power around $h = 9$; the lags-plus-met
model, armed with pressure gradients, wind fields, and humidity, continues to
skill-beat persistence through $h = 17$.

At Irish stations ($\bar{\rho}_1 = 0.850$), lower persistence means that
persistence forecasts are easier to beat at all horizons, so XGBoost achieves
near-maximum $H^*$ even without meteorological inputs.  Six of the eight Irish
stations reach $H^*_{\text{strict}} = 24$~h with lags-only alone — a
\emph{ceiling effect} that constrains $\Delta H^*$ to zero irrespective of
local $\rho_1$.  Where this ceiling does not apply — Dublin Airport, Henry
Street Limerick, and Madrid itself — $\Delta H^*$ is positive and increases
monotonically with $\rho_1$ (0, 6, and 8~h respectively, at $\rho_1 = 0.815$,
$0.876$, and $0.957$).  The apparent exception is Dundalk ($\rho_1 = 0.945$,
comparable to Madrid), which reaches $H^*_{\text{strict}} = 24$~h for the
lags-only model and thus has $\Delta H^* = 0$ by the ceiling constraint, not
because meteorology is uninformative.

Figure~\ref{fig:rho1_delta} shows the scatter of $\rho_1$ against
$\Delta H^*_{\text{strict}}$ across all nine sites.  A positive OLS slope is
evident ($r = 0.58$, $p = 0.10$), though formal significance is not achieved
at $\alpha = 0.05$ — primarily because the ceiling effect at six Irish
stations collapses their $\Delta H^*$ to zero regardless of $\rho_1$, reducing
effective degrees of freedom.  The relationship among the four unconstrained
sites is strong and consistent with the proposed mechanism.  Larger multi-city
studies varying systematically in $\rho_1$ are needed to establish the
association definitively.

The physical basis of this statistical pattern lies in boundary-layer dynamics.
Madrid Casa de Campo is a continental urban-background site characterised by
frequent anticyclonic blocking episodes, especially in autumn and winter,
during which a shallow, stable mixing layer traps \PMten{} near the surface
for periods of 12--36~h.  Under such conditions the aerosol loading evolves
slowly and is effectively ``remembered'' by the lagged concentration series for
many hours; the persistence forecast exploits this memory efficiently.  Only
when the boundary layer deepens or a synoptic disturbance (frontal passage,
precipitation event) forces rapid dilution does the lags-only model lose its
edge and meteorological covariates — particularly mean sea-level pressure,
wind speed, and relative humidity — provide the additional predictive
information captured by $\Delta H^* = +8$~h.  Ireland, by contrast, is
dominated by maritime westerlies that sustain deeper mixing and more frequent
precipitation scavenging, resulting in lower background autocorrelation and
allowing XGBoost to beat persistence over the full 24-h horizon without
meteorological assistance.

This mechanism has a concrete operational implication: the benefit of investing
in meteorological data pipelines depends on the local boundary-layer persistence
regime, well-proxied by $\rho_1$ of the hourly \PMten{} series.  Sites with
$\rho_1 \gtrsim 0.95$ — indicative of stable, slow-ventilating continental
boundary layers — are prime candidates for meteorologically enriched
forecasting; more maritime or convective sites with $\rho_1 < 0.87$ and
sufficient lags-only skill may not justify the added data complexity.

\subsection{Comparison with the literature}

Our Madrid finding of a large meteorology benefit ($\Delta H^*_{\text{strict}}
= +8$~h) is consistent with studies that report substantial gains from
meteorological covariates in continental, stagnation-prone environments where
aerosol concentrations are highly persistent.  The finding is specific,
however, to the medium-range horizon ($h = 9$--$17$~h); at short horizons
($h \le 8$) the DM-HLN test fails to detect significant differences.
\citet{Bai2018} similarly found that lagged concentrations are competitive
with meteorological models at sub-daily scales, suggesting that the
information content of concurrent meteorology is most valuable at lead times
where the lagged signal has sufficiently decayed.

The Irish results, showing negligible aggregate benefit, are consistent with
studies in more maritime or well-ventilated environments where boundary-layer
mixing limits pollution persistence.  Notably, the
two exceptions within the Irish network — Henry Street Limerick
($\Delta H^*_{\text{strict}} = +6$~h) and Dublin Airport ($+1$~h) — are
stations with distinct local characteristics (traffic canyon and airport
boundary-layer effects, respectively) that may produce more persistent local
concentration dynamics.

SARIMA performance merits comment.  At Madrid, SARIMA reaches
$H^*_{\text{strict}} = 8$~h (one hour less than lags-only XGBoost), which
is competitive for a purely linear model.  In Ireland, however, SARIMA
is on average considerably weaker (mean $H^*_{\text{strict}} = 14.3$~h vs.\
22.0~h for lags-only XGBoost), though it remains a useful benchmark.  The
SARIMA underperformance at some Irish stations (particularly Henry Street
Limerick, $H^*_{\text{strict}} = 4$~h) may reflect non-stationary dynamics
that violate the model's linearity and stationarity assumptions.

\subsection{Limitations and future directions}

Several limitations constrain the scope of our conclusions.  First, our
meteorological covariates are \emph{concurrent observations}, not forecasts;
in a fully operational setting, forecast-horizon errors in the meteorological
driver would reduce the advantage of lags-plus-met models.  Quantifying this
\emph{meteorological forecast cascade} is an important next step.

Second, the Ireland analysis pools stations with heterogeneous emissions
profiles.  A station-stratified analysis linking autocorrelation explicitly to
local traffic intensity, spatial geometry, or sea-proximity could sharpen
the mechanistic interpretation offered here.

Third, we restrict model training to fixed hyperparameters and a single
XGBoost architecture.  Neural network approaches that jointly model multiple
horizons (e.g., temporal fusion transformers) may exhibit different
autocorrelation-regime sensitivity and warrant separate investigation.

Finally, the study period includes the COVID-19 lockdown years (2020--2021),
which may have altered traffic-related \PMten{} dynamics.  Sensitivity
analyses excluding those years were not performed but would strengthen
robustness claims.

% ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
\section{Conclusions}
\label{sec:conclusions}
% ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

This study quantified the value of meteorological covariates for extending the
useful forecast horizon $H^*$ of hourly \PMten{} at two European sites using a
rigorous rolling-origin backtesting protocol with XGBoost and SARIMA models.

The main findings are:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Meteorology can substantially extend $H^*$, but only in
    high-autocorrelation regimes.}  At Madrid Casa de Campo
    ($\rho_1 = 0.957$), adding nine meteorological covariates extended
    $H^*_{\text{strict}}$ from 9~h to 17~h ($\Delta H^* = +8$~h).  At eight
    Irish stations ($\bar{\rho}_1 = 0.850$) the mean gain was only $+0.9$~h.

  \item \textbf{The meteorology benefit is horizon-specific.}  At Madrid,
    the gain is concentrated in the $h = 9$--$17$~h window and is
    statistically significant only at $h = 12$ (DM-HLN, $p = 0.012$).
    At shorter and longer horizons the benefit is statistically
    indistinguishable from zero.

  \item \textbf{The autocorrelation-persistence mechanism governs the regime
    transition.}  When autocorrelation is high, persistence is a formidable
    baseline at short leads, and meteorology fills the gap that lags-only
    models leave open at medium leads.  When autocorrelation is moderate,
    lags already beat persistence across the full horizon and meteorology
    adds little.

  \item \textbf{SARIMA is a competitive linear reference but does not
    systematically outperform XGBoost.}  Its $H^*_{\text{strict}}$ is
    comparable to lags-only XGBoost at Madrid (8~h vs.\ 9~h) but weaker on
    average across Ireland (14.3~h vs.\ 22.0~h).

\end{enumerate}

From an operational perspective, the findings suggest that the investment in
real-time meteorological data pipelines is well-justified for \PMten{}
monitoring sites characterised by high lag-1 autocorrelation ($\rho_1
\gtrsim 0.95$), indicative of continental, stagnation-prone boundary layers.
For sites with more moderate persistence ($\rho_1 < 0.90$), simpler
lags-only models may already capture the bulk of predictable signal, and the
added complexity of meteorological co-predictors — including the requirement
for NWP forecast drivers in an operational setting — may not translate into
meaningful horizon gains.

The tightened \PMten{} limit established by EU Directive 2024/2881/EU makes
accurate medium-horizon (12--18~h) forecasting increasingly valuable for
episode management and population protection.  Our results indicate that
meteorologically enriched models can provide exactly this capability at suitable
sites, and that lag-1 autocorrelation provides a simple, readily computable
indicator for identifying those sites.
