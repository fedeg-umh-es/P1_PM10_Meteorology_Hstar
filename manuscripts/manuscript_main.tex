\begin{abstract}
\noindent
Multi-horizon forecasts of urban \PMten{} are increasingly relevant as ambient
standards tighten.  We ask whether adding meteorological predictors increases
the range of lead times over which a model shows positive skill relative to a
naïve persistence baseline.  We use a rolling-origin, expanding-window
backtesting design over January 2020--July 2023 across nine monitoring sites:
Madrid Casa de Campo (Spain) and eight stations in Ireland.  We compare
persistence, SARIMA(1,0,1)(1,0,0)$_{24}$, and direct multi-horizon XGBoost trained
separately for each $h \in \{1,\ldots,24\}$, using either \PMten{} lags only
(\textit{lags only}) or lags plus nine meteorological covariates observed at
the forecast origin $t$ (\textit{lags + met.}); meteorological predictors are
not drawn from the target time $t+h$ or any other future point.

Forecast quality is summarised by the RMSE skill score relative to persistence.
Our primary horizon metric, $H^*_{\mathrm{strict,max-run}}$, is defined as the
\emph{length} (in hours) of the longest contiguous run of strictly positive
skill, located anywhere within $h=1,\ldots,24$ (e.g., a run $h=3$--$11$ has
length 9 and terminal horizon $h=11$).  We additionally report
$H^*_{\mathrm{strict,from-}h1}$ (positive-skill run starting at $h=1$) and the
relaxed last-positive horizon $H^*_{\mathrm{relax}}$.

At Madrid, XGBoost (lags only) attains its longest positive-skill run over
$h=3$--$11$ ($H^*_{\mathrm{strict,max-run}}=9$~h), whereas adding meteorology yields
a run over $h=1$--$17$ ($H^*_{\mathrm{strict,max-run}}=17$~h), an eight-hour
increase in maximum run length.  Across Ireland, the mean increase in
$H^*_{\mathrm{strict,max-run}}$ is $+1.0$~h.  DM-HLN tests at
$h\in\{1,6,12,24\}$ indicate that, for Madrid, the lags+met.\ advantage is
statistically significant only at $h=12$ ($p=0.012$; nominal and consistent with
a four-test Bonferroni threshold of 0.0125).  A moving-block bootstrap over
forecast origins shows that the Madrid $\Delta H^*_{\mathrm{strict,max-run}}$
point estimate carries a wide 95\% interval that includes zero, so this
result should be read as directional rather than precise (see
Section~\ref{sec:results}).  An exploratory across-site
analysis relating lag-1 autocorrelation to $\Delta H^*$ is not statistically
significant ($r=0.555$, $p=0.121$, $n=9$).

Overall, the observed site contrast is compatible with meteorology providing
additional information primarily where persistence is harder to beat at short
leads, but the evidence is limited by the small number of sites, ceiling
censoring at 24~h, and the restricted set of inferential tests.  Operational
interpretation depends on the time alignment and availability of the
meteorological predictors.

\medskip
\noindent
\textbf{Keywords:} PM\textsubscript{10}; atmospheric aerosol; meteorological
covariates; forecast horizon; XGBoost; SARIMA; skill score; Diebold--Mariano
test; air quality.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}
% ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

Particulate matter with aerodynamic diameter below 10~\si{\micro\metre}
(\PMten{}) is the dominant coarse aerosol fraction in the urban boundary layer
and one of the most consequential atmospheric pollutants in European cities.
Its surface concentration integrates contributions from primary crustal and
traffic-related emissions, long-range transported dust, and secondary aerosol
formation, all modulated by boundary-layer mixing, wet scavenging, and
synoptic-scale advection \citep{Seinfeld2006, Kumar2016}.  Sustained exposure
is associated with cardiovascular and respiratory morbidity, premature
mortality, and reduced quality of life \citep{WHO2021, EEA2023}.  The European
regulatory framework has progressively tightened in response: Directive
2008/50/EC established the current limit of
40~\si{\micro\gram\per\cubic\metre} as an annual mean, and the revised
Directive 2024/2881/EU, which entered into force in December 2024, halves that
threshold to 20~\si{\micro\gram\per\cubic\metre} by 2030 \citep{EU2024_2881}.
Against this backdrop, accurate \PMten{} forecasts are increasingly valuable
both for triggering episode-specific abatement measures and for guiding
longer-term urban planning.

The temporal evolution of near-surface \PMten{} is tightly coupled to
boundary-layer dynamics.  Under stable nocturnal or anticyclonic conditions,
the mixing layer is shallow and aerosol concentrations build up through
in-situ accumulation, producing high lag-1 autocorrelation.  Conversely,
deep daytime convective mixing, frontal passages, and precipitation scavenging
all accelerate aerosol dispersal, reducing persistence.  Wind speed and
direction influence horizontal transport of aerosol plumes from upwind sources,
while relative humidity modulates hygroscopic growth and related aerosol
properties \citep{Seinfeld2006, Kumar2016}.  This physical coupling motivates
considering meteorological predictors as potential covariates in statistical and
machine-learning forecasting models, particularly at lead times where the
information content of recent \PMten{} history alone may decay.

Published results vary across locations, model classes, predictor sets and
forecast horizons, making direct comparison difficult.  For example,
\citet{Pak2018} report improvements when boundary-layer predictors are included
for PM prediction in Korea, and \citet{Bai2018} consider wavelet-based
feature construction with meteorological inputs.  In other settings, lagged
pollutant concentrations already capture much of the predictable variance at
sub-daily horizons, leaving limited scope for additional covariates.

The \emph{forecast horizon problem} is central to operational decision-making in
air-quality management.  Practitioners need to know not only whether a model is
skilful on average, but where (in lead time) skill is positive relative to a
naïve benchmark.  We therefore summarise horizon-dependent skill using $H^*$
metrics based on contiguous runs of positive skill (defined precisely in
Section~\ref{sec:methods}).  The central question of this paper is whether, and
by how much, adding meteorological covariates increases the maximum length of a
positive-skill run for \PMten{} forecasting, and whether simple site-level
descriptors such as autocorrelation provide a plausible contextual explanation.

To address this question we conduct a controlled multi-site comparison.  Madrid
Casa de Campo (Spain) and a network of eight stations in Ireland provide
contrasting settings with different \PMten{} persistence regimes.  We evaluate
persistence, SARIMA, and XGBoost under a rolling-origin protocol intended to
preserve temporal ordering between training and evaluation, and we use the
Diebold--Mariano test \citep{Diebold1995, Harvey1997} to assess whether selected
horizon-specific skill differences are statistically distinguishable.

The remainder of this paper is structured as follows.  Section~\ref{sec:data}
describes the datasets and preprocessing.  Section~\ref{sec:methods} presents
the backtesting protocol, model specifications, and evaluation metrics.
Section~\ref{sec:results} reports skill curves, $H^*$ estimates, and
significance tests for both sites.  Section~\ref{sec:discussion} interprets
the results in terms of the autocorrelation-persistence mechanism.
Section~\ref{sec:conclusions} summarises the main findings and their
operational implications.

\section{Data}
\label{sec:data}
% ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

\subsection{Madrid}

Hourly \PMten{} concentrations and concurrent meteorological measurements were
obtained for Madrid Casa de Campo from the Madrid City Council open-data portal.
The station is classified as an urban-background site on the western edge of
the city, away from major point sources, and is representative of citywide
background conditions.  The study period spans January 2020 through July 2023;
records with missing \PMten{} or meteorological data were flagged and excluded
from model training and evaluation on a per-origin basis.  Nine meteorological
variables were retained: precipitation (\textit{rain}), temperature
(\textit{temp}), wet-bulb temperature (\textit{wetb}), dew-point temperature
(\textit{dewpt}), vapour pressure (\textit{vappr}), relative humidity
(\textit{rhum}), mean sea-level pressure (\textit{msl}), wind speed
(\textit{wdsp}), and wind direction (\textit{wddir}).

The lag-1 autocorrelation of hourly \PMten{} at Madrid over the training
period (2020--2022) is $\rho_1 = 0.957$, indicative of a highly persistent
series dominated by slow, boundary-layer-driven accumulation and dispersal
episodes lasting tens of hours.

\subsection{Ireland}

Hourly \PMten{} concentrations and meteorological covariates were obtained from
the Irish Environmental Protection Agency (EPA) monitoring network.  Eight
stations distributed across Ireland were selected on the basis of data
completeness over the study window (January 2020 -- July 2023):
Birr (Co.\ Offaly), Dublin Airport, Dundalk (Co.\ Louth), Pearse Street
Dublin, Ringsend Dublin, Edenderry (Co.\ Offaly), Henry Street Limerick, and
Portlaoise (Co.\ Laois).  A ninth candidate station, Rathmines Dublin, was
excluded prior to analysis due to data quality issues (121 negative \PMten{}
readings and maximum values exceeding
500~\si{\micro\gram\per\cubic\metre}).  The same nine meteorological variables
used for Madrid were extracted from the nearest EPA synoptic station for each
monitoring site.

\section{Methods}
\label{sec:methods}
% ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

\subsection{Backtesting protocol}

We adopt a rolling-origin, expanding-window backtesting scheme intended to
preserve temporal ordering between training and evaluation.  For each forecast
origin $t$, the training window covers all available observations up to (but
not including) $t$.  The forecast origin advances in nominal daily strides of
24~h, producing one $h = 1, \ldots, 24$ forecast vector per origin.

The evaluation period is 1 January -- 31 July 2023 for the Irish network and
1 January -- 30 December 2023 for Madrid; the two sites were evaluated over
different windows, yielding 362 origins for Madrid and between 145 (Dublin
Airport) and 212 origins per Irish station, before further row-level
exclusions.  The number of valid forecast--observation pairs varied by
station and horizon after applying station-specific data-availability and
complete-case requirements; for Madrid, paired lags-only/lags+meteorology
samples used in the DM-HLN tests were $n=354$, $356$, $346$ and $354$ at
$h=1,6,12,24$ respectively (Table~\ref{tab:madrid_dm}).

Model classes, hyperparameters and feature sets were fixed prior to the
reported evaluation window.

\subsection{Model specifications}

\paragraph{Persistence.}
The persistence model predicts $\hat{y}_{t+h} = y_{t-1}$ for all $h$, where
$y_{t-1}$ is the last \PMten{} observation available before the forecast
origin $t$ (the training window used to construct this and all other
models' inputs excludes $t$ itself).  It serves as the denominator benchmark
for the skill score.

\paragraph{SARIMA.}
We fit a SARIMA(1,0,1)(1,0,0)$_{24}$ model at each origin using the
\textit{statsmodels} implementation of exact maximum likelihood.  To avoid
numerical instability of the Kalman filter on very long training windows, the
training series is truncated to the most recent 17\,520 observations
(approximately two calendar years) at each origin.  Multi-step-ahead forecasts
are generated by iterating the fitted state-space model forward.  SARIMA is
treated as a univariate statistical reference; it does not receive
meteorological inputs.

\paragraph{XGBoost — lags only.}
A separate XGBoost regressor \citep{Chen2016} is trained for each horizon
$h \in \{1, \ldots, 24\}$ (the \emph{direct} multi-horizon strategy).  The
feature set consists of eight lagged \PMten{} values
($y_{t-1}, y_{t-2}, y_{t-3}, y_{t-6}, y_{t-12}, y_{t-24}, y_{t-48},
y_{t-168}$) and four calendar indicators (hour of day, day of week, month,
Julian day).  Hyperparameters are fixed across all origins and horizons
(Table~\ref{tab:xgb_params}) to ensure fair comparison; no origin-level
cross-validation is performed.  The most recent lagged \PMten{} value
available to the model is $y_{t-1}$ (one hour before the forecast origin);
$y_t$ itself is not included as a feature.  This is symmetric with the
persistence baseline, whose anchor value is likewise the last observation
strictly before $t$.

\paragraph{XGBoost — lags + meteorology.}
Identical to the lags-only model except that nine meteorological covariates,
measured at the forecast origin $t$, are added to the feature vector; no
meteorological value at or after $t+1$ is used as a predictor for any
horizon.  The operational interpretation nonetheless depends on the
availability of meteorology at forecast issue time in a deployed system.

Because the meteorological covariates used here are observations rather than
numerical weather forecasts, the reported performance should be interpreted
as retrospective and as an upper bound relative to an implementation that
would require NWP inputs.

\subsection{Evaluation metrics}

\paragraph{Skill score.}
For each model $m$ and horizon $h$, the RMSE skill score relative to
persistence is:
\begin{equation}
  S_m(h) = 1 - \frac{\mathrm{RMSE}_m(h)}{\mathrm{RMSE}_{\text{pers}}(h)},
  \label{eq:skill}
\end{equation}
where the RMSEs are computed over all forecast origins in the evaluation period.
Positive $S_m(h)$ indicates that model $m$ outperforms persistence at horizon
$h$.

\paragraph{Useful forecast horizon $H^*$.}
Skill sign alone does not imply statistical significance, but it provides a
simple horizon-by-horizon summary relative to the persistence baseline.
We define three related $H^*$ metrics (all in hours over $h\in\{1,\ldots,24\}$):
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{$H^*_{\mathrm{strict,max-run}}$} (primary): the \emph{length} of the
    longest contiguous run for which $S_m(h) > 0$ at every horizon in the run.
    The run may start after $h=1$.
  \item \textbf{$H^*_{\mathrm{strict,from-}h1}$} (auxiliary): the length of the
    uninterrupted positive-skill run starting specifically at $h=1$.
  \item \textbf{$H^*_{\mathrm{relax}}$}: the last horizon with $S_m(h) > 0$,
    allowing intermittent sign changes.
\end{itemize}
A run over $h=3,\ldots,11$ has length 9 and terminal horizon $h=11$; these
quantities should not be conflated.  Values with $S_m(h)=0$ are treated as
non-positive when identifying strict runs.

We summarise the meteorology effect via $\Delta H^* =
H^*_{\text{lags+met}} - H^*_{\text{lags only}}$ under the primary
$H^*_{\mathrm{strict,max-run}}$ criterion.

\paragraph{Diebold--Mariano test.}
Horizon-specific significance of the difference in squared forecast errors
between lags-only and lags-plus-meteorology is assessed using the
Diebold--Mariano test \citep{Diebold1995} with the finite-sample correction of
\citet{Harvey1997} (DM-HLN).  Tests are conducted at
$h\in\{1,6,12,24\}$ with two-sided $\alpha=0.05$.  Because four tests are
conducted per site, we also report against the Bonferroni-adjusted threshold
$\alpha_{\text{adj}}=0.0125$.  These four tests do not constitute a direct
inferential test of an entire $H^*$ run.

\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}
% ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

\subsection{Madrid: skill curves and $H^*$}

Figure~\ref{fig:madrid_skill} shows the RMSE skill score $S(h)$ as a function
of lead time for the three model classes at Madrid Casa de Campo.  The onset
and continuity of positive skill vary by model.  For XGBoost (lags only), the
longest contiguous positive-skill run occurs over $h=3$--$11$, giving
$H^*_{\mathrm{strict,max-run}}=9$~h (terminal horizon $h=11$) and
$H^*_{\mathrm{relax}}=15$~h.  SARIMA attains
$H^*_{\mathrm{strict,max-run}}=8$~h and $H^*_{\mathrm{relax}}=20$~h.

Adding meteorological covariates to XGBoost (\textit{lags + met.}) yields a
longest positive-skill run over $h=1$--$17$, giving
$H^*_{\mathrm{strict,max-run}}=17$~h (terminal horizon $h=17$) and
$H^*_{\mathrm{relax}}=17$~h.  Relative to lags only, this corresponds to an
eight-hour increase in maximum positive-skill run length
($\Delta H^*_{\mathrm{strict,max-run}}=+8$~h).  A moving-block bootstrap over
forecast origins (block length 7 days, 2000 resamples) places this estimate
within a wide 95\% interval (approximately $-7$ to $+12$~h), reflecting the
sensitivity of a run-length statistic to small perturbations near $S(h)=0$;
see Section~\ref{sec:discussion} for discussion.

Figure~\ref{fig:madrid_delta} plots the pointwise skill difference
$\Delta S(h) = S_{\text{lags+met}}(h) - S_{\text{lags only}}(h)$.  The
difference is positive at most horizons but grows substantially between $h = 9$
and $h = 17$, precisely the window where lags-only skill turns negative while
meteorology sustains it.  At $h = 18$ onwards both conditions are negative and
the gain dissipates.

Figure~\ref{fig:madrid_dm} and Table~\ref{tab:madrid_dm} report the DM-HLN
test results at $h \in \{1, 6, 12, 24\}$.  The only statistically significant
difference is at $h = 12$ ($\mathrm{DM} = 2.52$, $p = 0.012$), which
survives the Bonferroni-corrected threshold of $\alpha_{\text{adj}} = 0.0125$
for four simultaneous tests.  Lags-plus-meteorology is favoured at $h = 12$.
At $h = 1$ ($p = 0.243$), $h = 6$ ($p = 0.961$), and $h = 24$ ($p = 0.398$)
the null hypothesis of equal predictive accuracy cannot be rejected.  This
pattern is consistent with the $\Delta S(h)$ profile: the meteorology benefit
is concentrated in the mid-range, medium-term window.

\subsection{Ireland: station-level $H^*$ and meteorology benefit}

Figure~\ref{fig:ireland_skill} shows the RMSE skill curves for all eight Irish
stations as a 2$\times$4 panel grid.  A common pattern is visible: both the
lags-only and lags-plus-meteorology XGBoost models achieve positive skill across
most or all of the 1--24~h horizon at the majority of stations, in marked
contrast to Madrid where skill turned negative beyond $h \approx 9$--$11$.
The two curves are largely overlapping at most stations, foreshadowing the small
$\Delta H^*$ values reported below.

Figure~\ref{fig:ireland_delta} plots the pointwise meteorology benefit
$\Delta S(h) = S_{\text{lags+met}}(h) - S_{\text{lags only}}(h)$ for each
station.  Values cluster near zero across the full horizon range for most
stations, confirming that the lags-only model already captures the dominant
predictable variance.  Henry Street Limerick stands out as the station with
the largest positive $\Delta S$ at intermediate horizons, consistent with its
being the only station to show a notable $\Delta H^*_{\mathrm{strict,max-run}} = +7$~h.

Table~\ref{tab:ireland_hstar} reports $H^*_{\mathrm{strict,max-run}}$ and
$H^*_{\text{relax}}$ for all eight Irish stations and all four model classes
(evaluated under $H^*_{\text{strict,max-run}}$ from regenerated source datasets;
see Data Availability).
A striking feature is that both XGBoost conditions achieve near-maximum
$H^*_{\mathrm{strict,max-run}}$ at most stations (five of eight stations reach 24~h with
lags-only alone), leaving little room for meteorology to contribute.

The mean $\Delta H^*_{\mathrm{strict,max-run}}$ across the eight stations is
$\bar{\Delta H^*} = +1.0$~h ($\bar{H}^*_{\text{lags only}} = 21.9$~h,
$\bar{H}^*_{\text{lags+met}} = 22.9$~h), compared with $+8$~h at Madrid.  The two
exceptions are Dublin Airport ($\Delta H^*_{\mathrm{strict,max-run}} = +1$~h) and Henry
Street Limerick ($\Delta H^*_{\mathrm{strict,max-run}} = +7$~h); at the remaining six
stations the increment is zero.  Of these six, five are ceiling-constrained
(lags-only already achieves $H^*_{\mathrm{strict,max-run}} = 24$~h); Edenderry is the
exception, where both models achieve the same submaximal run length
($H^*_{\mathrm{strict,max-run}} = 16$~h), suggesting low local predictability rather
than a ceiling effect.  SARIMA performance is more variable: mean
$H^*_{\mathrm{strict,max-run}} = 13.9$$^b$~h, with a low of 4~h at Henry Street Limerick and
a high of 23$^b$~h at Dublin Airport.  Figure~\ref{fig:ireland_hstar} presents
these results as a grouped bar chart, facilitating direct station-by-station
comparison.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}
% ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

\subsection{The autocorrelation-persistence mechanism}

The central contrast between Madrid and Ireland — $\Delta H^*_{\mathrm{strict,max-run}}
= +8$~h vs.\ $+1.0$~h — is consistent with a possible role of
lag-1 autocorrelation in modulating the meteorology benefit.  At Madrid ($\rho_1 = 0.957$) the
\PMten{} signal is highly persistent: the naïve persistence forecast is
difficult to beat at short lead times ($h = 1$--$8$) because the concentration
one hour hence is almost identical to the concentration now.  Only at
$h \ge 9$, when the persistence signal has sufficiently decayed, can
meteorological covariates provide additional predictive information beyond what
the lagged \PMten{} series alone could supply.  The lags-only XGBoost exhausts
its predictive power around $h = 9$; the lags-plus-met model, incorporating
mean sea-level pressure, wind speed and direction, and humidity, continues to
outperform persistence through $h = 17$.  As noted in
Section~\ref{sec:results}, this $+8$~h point estimate carries a wide
bootstrap interval (approximately $-7$ to $+12$~h) and should be read as
directional rather than precise.

At Irish stations ($\bar{\rho}_1 = 0.850$), lower persistence means that
persistence forecasts are easier to beat at all horizons, so XGBoost achieves
near-maximum $H^*$ even without meteorological inputs.  Five of the eight Irish
stations reach $H^*_{\mathrm{strict,max-run}} = 24$~h with lags-only alone — a
\emph{ceiling effect} that constrains $\Delta H^*$ to zero irrespective of
local $\rho_1$.  A sixth station (Edenderry) also has $\Delta H^* = 0$ but at
a submaximal level ($H^*_{\mathrm{strict,max-run}} = 16$~h for both conditions),
indicating low predictability rather than a ceiling constraint.
Where the ceiling does not apply — Dublin Airport, Henry
Street Limerick, and Madrid itself — $\Delta H^*$ is positive and increases
monotonically with $\rho_1$ ($+1$, $+7$, and $+8$~h respectively, at
$\rho_1 = 0.815$, $0.876$, and $0.957$).  Henry Street Limerick's $+7$~h
estimate, like Madrid's, shows substantial resampling uncertainty under the
same bootstrap procedure, so its magnitude should likewise be read with
caution even though its direction is consistent with the mechanism proposed
here.  The apparent exception is Dundalk ($\rho_1 = 0.945$,
comparable to Madrid), which reaches $H^*_{\mathrm{strict,max-run}} = 24$~h for the
lags-only model and thus has $\Delta H^* = 0$ by the ceiling constraint, not
because meteorology is uninformative.

Figure~\ref{fig:rho1_delta} shows the scatter of $\rho_1$ against
$\Delta H^*_{\mathrm{strict,max-run}}$ across all nine sites.  A positive OLS slope is
evident ($r = 0.555$, $p = 0.121$, $n = 9$), though formal significance is not achieved
at $\alpha = 0.05$ — primarily because the ceiling effect at five Irish
stations (plus Edenderry's submaximal tie) collapses their $\Delta H^*$ to
zero regardless of $\rho_1$, reducing
effective degrees of freedom.  The relationship among the four unconstrained
sites is strong and consistent with the proposed mechanism.  Larger multi-city
studies varying systematically in $\rho_1$ are needed to establish the
association definitively.

Table~\ref{tab:rho1} (extending the primary metric with the from-$h{=}1$ diagnostic) reports the same $\rho_1$--$\Delta H^*$ relationship shown in Figure~\ref{fig:rho1_delta} in tabular form. Notably, Madrid and Henry St.\ Limerick are the only two sites showing a from-$h{=}1$ gain (0$\to$17 and 1$\to$24 respectively), a pattern consistent with the autocorrelation narrative. Conversely, Dublin Airport fails to establish a continuous run from $h{=}1$ (0/0), indicating its positive skill begins later in the horizon.

A plausible physical interpretation of this statistical pattern involves boundary-layer dynamics.
Madrid Casa de Campo is a continental urban-background site characterised by
frequent anticyclonic blocking episodes, especially in autumn and winter,
during which a shallow, stable mixing layer traps \PMten{} near the surface
for periods of 12--36~h.  Under such conditions the aerosol loading evolves
slowly and is effectively ``remembered'' by the lagged concentration series for
many hours; the persistence forecast exploits this memory efficiently.  When
the boundary layer deepens or a synoptic disturbance forces rapid dilution,
the lags-only model may lose its edge and meteorological covariates —
particularly mean sea-level pressure, wind speed, and relative humidity —
may provide additional predictive information, consistent with the observed
$\Delta H^* = +8$~h.  However, this study does not include direct measurements
of boundary-layer height, stability indices, or synoptic circulation
classification, so this interpretation remains a hypothesis.
Ireland, by contrast, is
dominated by maritime westerlies that sustain deeper mixing and more frequent
precipitation scavenging, resulting in lower background autocorrelation and
allowing XGBoost to beat persistence over the full 24-h horizon without
meteorological assistance.

SARIMA performance merits comment.  A post-hoc computational audit
identified and corrected a one-step scoring-alignment error in the SARIMA
forecasts (each prediction had been compared against the target timestamp
one hour later than the one it actually targeted); the XGBoost
lags-only/lags+meteorology comparison above, this paper's primary result,
was unaffected by that error.  At Madrid, SARIMA reaches
$H^*_{\mathrm{strict,max-run}} = 8$~h ($H^*_{\mathrm{relax}}=20$~h; one hour
less than lags-only XGBoost), which is competitive for a purely linear
model; the one-hour realignment did not cross a sign change in the Madrid
SARIMA skill curve, so this figure is unchanged by the correction.  In
Ireland, however, SARIMA is on average considerably weaker (corrected mean
$H^*_{\mathrm{strict}} \approx 13.9$$^b$~h vs.\ 21.9~h for lags-only
XGBoost), though it remains a useful benchmark.  Four stations' SARIMA
figures changed under the correction: Birr's $H^*_{\mathrm{relax}}$ fell
from 24~h to 16~h; Pearse Street Dublin's $H^*_{\mathrm{strict}}$ and
$H^*_{\mathrm{relax}}$ both fell to 15~h (from 17~h and 23~h respectively);
Dublin Airport's $H^*_{\mathrm{strict}}$ and $H^*_{\mathrm{relax}}$, and
Dundalk's $H^*_{\mathrm{relax}}$, are lower bounds of 23~h (from a
previously reported 24~h), pending confirmation of the still-unrecovered
horizon-24 forecast (Dundalk's $H^*_{\mathrm{strict}}$ is unaffected and
remains 18~h).  The SARIMA underperformance at some Irish stations
(particularly Henry Street Limerick, $H^*_{\text{strict}} = 4$~h, also
unaffected by the correction) may reflect non-stationary dynamics that
violate the model's linearity and stationarity assumptions.

The meteorology benefit is associated with the local boundary-layer
persistence regime, proxied by $\rho_1$ of the hourly \PMten{} series: it is
larger at high-$\rho_1$ sites (stable, slow-ventilating continental boundary
layers) and smaller at more maritime or convective sites where lags-only skill
is already high. These gains represent a retrospective upper bound on operational value (see Section 5.3).

\subsection{Comparison with the literature}

Our Madrid finding of a large meteorology benefit ($\Delta H^*_{\mathrm{strict,max-run}}
= +8$~h) is consistent with studies that report substantial gains from
meteorological covariates in continental, stagnation-prone environments where
aerosol concentrations are highly persistent.  The finding is specific,
however, to the medium-range horizon ($h = 9$--$17$~h); at short horizons
($h \le 8$) the DM-HLN test fails to detect significant differences.
\citet{Bai2018} similarly found that lagged concentrations are competitive
with meteorological models at sub-daily scales, suggesting that the
information content of concurrent meteorology is most valuable at lead times
where the lagged signal has sufficiently decayed.

The Irish results, showing negligible aggregate benefit, are consistent with
studies in more maritime or well-ventilated environments where boundary-layer
mixing limits pollution persistence.  Notably, the
two exceptions within the Irish network — Henry Street Limerick
($\Delta H^*_{\mathrm{strict,max-run}} = +7$~h) and Dublin Airport ($+1$~h) — are
stations with distinct local characteristics (traffic canyon and airport
boundary-layer effects, respectively) that may produce more persistent local
concentration dynamics.

\subsection{Limitations and future directions}

Several limitations constrain the scope of our conclusions.  First, our
meteorological covariates are \emph{concurrent observations}, not origin-time forecasts.
Because of this, the gains reported here represent a retrospective upper bound rather than
demonstrated operational value. In a fully operational setting, forecast-horizon errors
in the meteorological driver would reduce the advantage of lags-plus-met models.
Quantifying this \emph{meteorological forecast cascade} is an important next step.

Second, the Ireland analysis pools stations with heterogeneous emissions
profiles.  A station-stratified analysis linking autocorrelation explicitly to
local traffic intensity, spatial geometry, or sea-proximity could sharpen
the mechanistic interpretation offered here.

Third, we restrict model training to fixed hyperparameters and a single
XGBoost architecture.  Neural network approaches that jointly model multiple
horizons (e.g., temporal fusion transformers) may exhibit different
autocorrelation-regime sensitivity and warrant separate investigation.

Fourth, $H^*_{\mathrm{strict,max-run}}$ is a run-length statistic and is
therefore sensitive to small perturbations in the skill curve near
$S(h)=0$.  A moving-block bootstrap over forecast origins (block length 7
days, 2000 resamples) shows that Madrid's headline $\Delta H^*_{\mathrm{strict,max-run}}
= +8$~h point estimate carries a 95\% interval of approximately $-7$ to
$+12$~h, and that Henry Street Limerick's $+7$~h shows comparable
resampling uncertainty.  Both intervals include values at or below zero.
The direction of the meteorology effect at these two sites is consistent
across the point estimate and the bulk of the bootstrap distribution, but
the exact magnitude should not be treated as a precise quantity; we
recommend that any operational or editorial use of these numbers cite the
interval, not only the point estimate.

Finally, the study period includes the COVID-19 lockdown years (2020--2021),
which may have altered traffic-related \PMten{} dynamics.  Sensitivity
analyses excluding those years were not performed but would strengthen
robustness claims.

A further transparency note: the Irish station evaluations reported here were
regenerated from recovered source datasets because the row-level prediction
files from the original computational run were not retained.  The regeneration
used the same code, configuration, hyperparameters, and random seeds as the
original run.  Equivalence was verified by comparing the regenerated
$H^*_{\text{strict}}$ and DM statistics against the values reported in the
original analysis log; all matched within rounding tolerance.  Details are
provided in the Data Availability statement.

\section{Conclusions}
\label{sec:conclusions}
% ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

This study quantified the value of meteorological covariates for extending the
useful forecast horizon $H^*$ of hourly \PMten{} across nine monitoring sites
in two European settings using a
rigorous rolling-origin backtesting protocol with XGBoost and SARIMA models.

The main findings are:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Meteorology can substantially extend $H^*$, but only in
    high-autocorrelation regimes.}  At Madrid Casa de Campo
    ($\rho_1 = 0.957$), adding nine meteorological covariates extended
    $H^*_{\text{strict}}$ from 9~h to 17~h ($\Delta H^* = +8$~h).  At eight
    Irish stations ($\bar{\rho}_1 = 0.850$) the mean gain was only $+1.0$~h.
    These point estimates carry substantial resampling uncertainty (a
    moving-block bootstrap places Madrid's $\Delta H^*$ within a 95\%
    interval of approximately $-7$ to $+12$~h; see
    Sections~\ref{sec:results} and~\ref{sec:discussion}); the direction of
    the effect is robust, its exact magnitude is not.

  \item \textbf{The meteorology benefit is horizon-specific.}  At Madrid,
    the gain is concentrated in the $h = 9$--$17$~h window and is
    statistically significant only at $h = 12$ (DM-HLN, $p = 0.012$).
    At shorter and longer horizons the benefit is statistically
    indistinguishable from zero.

  \item \textbf{The autocorrelation-persistence interpretation is consistent
    with the observed regime contrast, but remains a hypothesis.}  When autocorrelation is high, persistence is a formidable
    baseline at short leads, and meteorology fills the gap that lags-only
    models leave open at medium leads.  When autocorrelation is moderate,
    lags already beat persistence across the full horizon and meteorology
    adds little.

  \item \textbf{SARIMA is a competitive linear reference but does not
    systematically outperform XGBoost.}  Its $H^*_{\mathrm{strict}}$ is
    comparable to lags-only XGBoost at Madrid (8~h vs.\ 9~h, confirmed
    after correcting a one-step scoring-alignment error identified during
    a post-hoc computational audit) but weaker on average across Ireland
    ($\approx$13.9~h vs.\ 21.9~h; Section~\ref{sec:discussion}).

\end{enumerate}

Interpreted within the limits of a retrospective upper bound (see Section 5.3),
these results suggest a hypothesis for operational prioritisation:
meteorologically enriched \PMten{} forecasting is most likely to be worthwhile
at monitoring sites with high lag-1 autocorrelation ($\rho_1 \gtrsim 0.95$),
indicative of continental, stagnation-prone boundary layers, whereas sites with
more moderate persistence ($\rho_1 < 0.90$) may already capture the bulk of
predictable signal with lags-only models.  Testing this hypothesis operationally
would require replacing observed meteorology with forecasts available at the
\PMten{} forecast origin, including their issue time, forecast cycle, lead time
and latency.

The tightened \PMten{} limit established by EU Directive 2024/2881/EU makes
accurate medium-horizon (12--18~h) forecasting increasingly valuable for
episode management and population protection.  Our results suggest that
meteorologically enriched models may extend useful skill at suitable
sites, and that lag-1 autocorrelation may serve as a candidate screening
indicator, pending validation on independent datasets.
